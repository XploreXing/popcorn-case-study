The problem

You understood every joke. You learned nothing.

You watch an English YouTuber for half an hour and follow all of it. A few minutes later someone asks what new expressions you picked up, and nothing comes to mind.

Most learners blame their own discipline. I think the tools are at fault. Each way of watching fails for a different reason.

How you watchWhat happens
No captionsToo hard. Most people give up within minutes.
Captions in your languageYou follow the story and pick up no new words.
Captions in the language you're learningYou read instead of listening.
Flashcard appsWell organised, but the sentences have nothing to do with your life.

Fun content is hard to learn from. Content built for learning is rarely fun.

How do you turn fast, unscripted video into small things worth learning, without interrupting the video?

I used to work as an interpreter, so I get asked why anyone should learn a language now that AI translates everything. Translation gets business done. It doesn't let you get the joke first-hand. Popcorn Language uses AI to support the learner, and leaves the understanding to them.

The idea

Learning as snacking

Every feature name in the product comes from one picture: you're watching something good, and you're snacking. I chose it for three reasons, and each one answers a specific anxiety that language apps create.

Saving creates no homework

In a flashcard app, every word you save becomes a review due tomorrow. People stop saving. Bagging a snack owes you nothing.

A pantry has no overdue pile

Unreviewed cards build up, feel like debt, and people quit. A pouch only holds what you picked. The counter shows what you've collected and never what you've missed.

Relaxed people learn more

Krashen's affective filter hypothesis says anxiety blocks acquisition. Words like taste, bite and digest carry none of the classroom.

Ask for what people already know

Few learners know their CEFR level. Most know their IELTS, TOEFL or CET score. The form asks for the score and shows the level it maps to before anything is saved.

The level decides how hard every lesson will be, so it can only change once a week. The hint says so, and says how long is left.

Start from interest

A learning app usually opens with a syllabus. This one opens with a question about what you feel like watching. The metaphor starts here because this is where the mood is set.

The search button still says Search. The placeholder can play because the button next to it doesn't.

Keeping the wait calm

Lessons are generated on demand. The transcript is split into sections and written in batches, which brings the first expressions down to 15 to 30 seconds. That is still a wait, so the screen works to keep it calm: a line says something is being made for you, the progress bar keeps moving, and expressions appear as each batch finishes, so the list is never blank.

The bag button stays off until generation ends, and its tooltip says when it will work.

Switch to Before to read the first version. It said "validated", "chunk 2/5" and "fallback-generated". Those words describe the pipeline, and a learner has no use for them. The rewrite keeps one number: how many expressions are ready to read. Short batch shows the worst case.

One verb for the main action

"Bag it" replaced "Mark as wanna learn". It is two words, it names a physical action, and it promises nothing about studying later.

Each card answers three questions in the learner's own language: what it means here, how to use it, and why it's worth knowing. Switch the product language and the explanations follow, while the expression and its example stay in English.

In the app the button is an icon and this label is its tooltip. It is shown as text here so you can read it. Expressions are samples in the app's output format.

Voice and standards

Four rules any writer on the product can apply

A glossary keeps terms consistent. It doesn't tell the next writer what to do with a string nobody has written yet. These four rules do. Three of the four "don't" examples are strings the product used to ship.

1Plain first, playful second

If a snack word makes the action harder to guess, use the plain word. One snack word per string is enough.

Do
Bag it
Don't
Remake
"Remake" was meant to sound like the kitchen. Nobody guessed that it reloads the list, so the button now says "Try again".

2Count what's gained

Show totals and streaks. Never show what's overdue, unreviewed or missed.

Do
12 authentic expressions bagged
Don't
12 expressions waiting for review

3Say it like a friend on the sofa

Short sentences. "You" and "your". Contractions. Tell people what to do next, and skip the apology.

Do
Enter your email.
Don't
email is required

4Hide the machinery

Cache, chunk, fallback, validated, legacy: these are the team's words. If the reader can't act on a detail, leave it out.

Do
Older batch. Can't be bagged.
Don't
Legacy cached content can't be bagged

    The metaphor is rebuilt in each language

    • Chinese got lucky. 吃透, "to eat something through", is already the everyday idiom for understanding a thing fully, so 彻底吃透 is stronger than the English it came from.
    • "Snack Hoarder" became 零食大富翁 (snack tycoon) and 간식계 만수르, which borrows a Korean meme about a famously rich man. Neither is a translation. Both keep the joke.

    Sentences follow ICU MessageFormat

    Every string is one complete ICU message. Variables and links sit inside the sentence, so each language sets its own word order, punctuation and plural forms.

    The consent line on the sign-up form shows why this matters. English puts the verb first: "I agree to the Privacy Policy." Korean puts it last, closer to "To the Privacy Policy, I agree." The first version stored that sentence as two separate strings, and the code always printed them in the same order. English read fine. Korean came out verb-first, which is not how a Korean sentence is built. As one ICU message, each language places the link where its grammar needs it.

    Before · two strings joined in code

    EnglishI agree to thePrivacy Policy Reads right. Verb, then the thing agreed to.
    Korean동의합니다개인정보 처리방침 Reads wrong. "Agree" came first, and a Korean sentence ends with its verb.

    After · one ICU message per language

    EnglishI agree to the Privacy Policy I agree to the <link>Privacy Policy</link>

    AI workflow

    How the copy gets written with an AI agent

    I built Popcorn Language alone with an AI coding agent: more than 180 commits since May 2025. The interface copy for three languages came out of the same way of working. It runs in five steps.

    1. ExtractThe agent pulls every button label and message out of the front-end code into one file of keys per language. That file is the base for localization.
    2. BriefI write the brief as a prompt: the brand concept, which features speak in the snack metaphor, and which screens stay strictly functional.
    3. DraftFrom the brief, the agent drafts the message file for each language.
    4. CalibrateI read the drafts and adjust them by hand.
    5. AuditThe agent scans every file again. It looks for sentences that read badly, one concept named two ways on different pages, and a key that means different things in different languages. Then I adjust again.

    What comes next

    What this design hasn't proved yet

    Popcorn Language is in a closed beta, so there is no real user feedback yet. The voice comes from my own side of the screen: it is what I, as a learner, want a language product to sound like. That is a starting point and not evidence.

    If I take the product further, I would let users decide:

    • Find people who learn from video and A/B test the snack labels against plain ones.
    • Run short trials and surveys to hear how the labels read to them.
    • Track taps where the metaphor names an action, and compare click-through. Does "Bag it" get used more than "Save"?

    The copy should then change with the numbers.
